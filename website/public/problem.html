Problem statement

Low Earth orbit is
crowded and opaque

Drag eventually removes every uncontrolled object from low Earth orbit. When it does governs conjunction planning, re-entry risk on the ground and the long-term evolution of the debris population. The mechanics have been understood for decades. The inputs have not.

Unknowable atmosphere

Thermospheric density depends on solar and geomagnetic activity that cannot be forecast years ahead. A five-year lifetime forecast must assume something about a driver nobody can predict.

Unknown objects

The area-to-mass ratio sets the drag. In this cohort mass is missing for 39.8 % of objects and radar cross-section for 60.7 %. For most debris it must be sampled, not measured.

Survivors dominate

Between 56 % and 74 % of scored objects are still in orbit when the evaluation window ends. A metric computed only on objects that decayed answers a different question.

The population drifts

Launch practice and the solar cycle both move. A random split lets a model see the future regime it is then scored on.

Missingness encodes the outcome

Whether an object's cross-section was ever measured depends on how long it survived — adjusted odds ratio 3.59 (95 % CI [2.09, 6.17]). Using it as a feature leaks the answer.

Nobody reports the choice

Papers report the model that scored best on their test set. An operator must choose before the forecast period. Whether that choice survives is rarely measured.

The gap this project addresses

Evaluation practice, not just model accuracy

Published lifetime and re-entry predictors are usually evaluated on one historical window, with a single split and point losses, on objects that have element-set histories. Three properties of the real problem are then invisible: right-censoring, population drift, and the cost of selecting a model without seeing its future.
